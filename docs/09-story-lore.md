## 1. The Flux (what powers are)

**The flux is geological.** A seam of unclassified energy runs under the Canadian Shield — old rock, older than bones, and something in it that was never mapped because nothing about it shows up on instruments that weren't built by people who already knew. The seam doesn't radiate. It *soaks*.

**Flux follows water.** That's the load-bearing law of the whole setting. The seam's discharge doesn't spread evenly — it travels the watershed, concentrating wherever water gathers, moves, and remembers. Which means:

- The **Mississippi** is a conduit running straight through the seam's discharge basin.
- The **Chain of Lakes** is a capacitor bank — five linked basins holding charge in the middle of a city.
- Therefore **Minneapolis is the strongest urban flux field in North America.** Not New York. Not LA. A midwestern city of lakes, because the water got there first.
- Lake water *feels different* here, and everyone says so without knowing why — swimmers talk about Bde Maka Ska the way surfers talk about a break. The **shoreline is charged ground**: resonances set faster near it, run hotter on it, and the first-generation rule was simple: *don't touch the lakes when you're angry.*
- The people who lived on this watershed for millennia had older words for the places that hum. The mining didn't create the flux. It **uncorked** it. Dakota and Anishinaabe accounts of the humming places predate 1918 by centuries, were ignored as folklore by everyone who arrived later, and are — first-generation supers eventually admitted, quietly, over drinks — the most accurate early literature the field has.

## 2. The One-Resonance Law (the rules of powers)

A century of evidence, no confirmed exceptions — until the player character.

- **Prolonged flux exposure "sets" exactly one resonance per person.** One person, one power. It behaves like a law of nature, not a tendency.
- **Setting happens in adolescence or under trauma.** The body has to be finished enough to hold it and shaken enough to open. Most sets are teenage and quiet — a bad week, a strange month, and then you're a person who can do a thing.
- **Resonances run in families like left-handedness** — statistically, not reliably. (Frankie Larsen's "gravity ran in the family" is literal. So is Maggie's density — see §4.)
- **A set is permanent.** No known transfer, no swap, no second set. You can deepen a resonance for a lifetime; you cannot trade it in.
- **Nulls exist.** A small fraction of flux-exposed people never set — "blanks." Folk wisdom pities them. Folk wisdom, as usual, has it exactly backwards (see §5).
- **Cascades kill children.** A resonance that begins setting in a body too young doesn't stop — it *cascades*, cycling harder as the body fails to close around it. Pre-adolescent sets are the horror story of flux medicine: rare, always fatal, always fast. This is WHY the law says adolescence. This is also the entire reason the game's story exists (§5).
- **Burnout is real.** Adults who set violently (mass-trauma sets) can burn out — the resonance takes more than the body had. The first generation buried its burnouts quietly and lied on the certificates.

## 3. History: breach, silence, daylight

### 1918 — The breach
Iron Range mining broke the seam in 1918 — a deep drift under the Vermilion Range cut into rock that hummed. The same autumn, northern Minnesota burned: the great fires took Cloquet and a thousand square miles, and **the cover story wrote itself** — anything strange that year was smoke, grief, and war. The first supers were **miners and firefighters**: men and women who set under collapse and firestorm, who could suddenly hold a gallery roof or walk a burning ridge line. They told no one but each other.

### 1918–1953 — The quiet generation
The first generation stayed inside the two institutions that already knew how to keep a secret: **the unions and the small towns.** A miner who could hold rock was a safety asset the local would protect like a pension. The rules of that era — the **Range Rules** — were never written down:

1. *Don't set in public.*
2. *Don't sell your hands* (no company gets to own what you are).
3. *Bury your dead quiet.*
4. *The lake knows; nobody else needs to.*

### 1953 — The Ely Deep Flare (the covered-up event)
The event that welded the Range Rules into scripture. A drift collapse at the **Ely Deep** working trapped eleven men; the rescue crew went down into a gallery where the seam itself had been cut open by the fall. The flux surged the shaft, and the entire rescue crew **mass-set at once** — nine adults, violently, in the dark. Three burned out within the year. Two never came back up the same (the records say "gas"). The survivors, in the panic of nine new resonances firing in a confined space, **leveled the headframe** from below — the surface crew watched a steel tower sit down like a tired man.

Everyone had a reason to bury it: the company (liability), the union (jobs — a haunted mine is a closed mine), the families (fear of what the government would do with a list of names). The official cause was a gas explosion. The nine survivors and their families became the spine of first-generation culture: the proof that setting could be a wound, that a list of names was a loaded weapon, and that nobody — company, state, or church — could be trusted to hold one.

**One of the nine was Alfred St. Pierre — density — Maggie St. Pierre's grandfather.** The St. Pierres have been carrying the Range Rules south ever since. This is why Maggie (44) drinks where the old-timers drink, why the first generation treats her as one of their own two generations early, and why her rage at being called a hero is inherited, not personal: *heroes get lists made of them.*

### 1953–2006 — The long quiet
Supers urbanized with everyone else. The Cities filled with second- and third-generation flux families who kept the Rules out of habit more than conviction. The lakes became the open secret's living room — which is why the supers-only speakeasy, **The Understory**, opened where it did and stayed neutral for everyone: neutrality was a Range Rule wearing a cocktail dress.

### 2006 — The Camden Bridge failure (going public)
An aging rail-and-pedestrian bridge over the Mississippi at Camden let go on a September evening — and **fourteen flux-set commuters acted in open daylight, on a hundred camera phones.** A woman held a truss. A man froze the river slack. A teenager ran the deck eleven times. Nobody died, and nobody could unsee it. The quiet generation's century of discipline ended in ninety seconds of ordinary people refusing to watch their neighbors drown — which, the old-timers admit, is the only way it ever could have ended honorably.

### 2006–present — Daylight and its invoices
- **Registration fights.** The state blinked first (no mandatory registry — the Range families' lobbying was quiet and absolute), but the *private sector* never stopped wanting the list. Remember that sentence.
- **Paradigm Civic Systems** grew from a Twin Cities security-and-logistics contractor into the region's flux-adjacent everything-vendor: consulting, "integrity infrastructure," and eventually autonomous enforcement (§6).
- **The U of M became the flux-botany capital of the world, almost by accident.** The same horticulture program that bred the Honeycrisp ran watershed uptake studies and found that certain cultivars *hold charge* — flux-expressing crops that grow strange and generous near charged water. Nobody weaponized it; they made better tomatoes. Flux agriculture (see `02`'s farming/greenhouse systems) is the lore's gentlest gift: the seam that made all this trouble also makes the best growing soil on the continent, and half of Bee Toliver's garden network is quietly planted in University cultivars.
- **The supers economy normalized.** Powers became day jobs (an electrician who IS the meter; a PT with perfect hands). Which is the setting's whole thesis: *people, not weapons. Lives, not lore entries.*

## 4. Why Minnesota keeps its powers local

Frequently asked in-world, so canon answers: flux saturation falls off with distance from the watershed. A set resonance travels fine (you keep your power in Florida), but *setting* mostly happens here, along the water. Families who want kids to set — or want them never to — move accordingly, and both kinds of family live on the same blocks. The Cities are to supers what a border town is to language: everyone's a little bilingual.

## 5. The Anchor and The Act (the buried center of the game)

### Who he is
**Elias "Eli" Monroe, 39.** Single dad. Uptown. Runs the base, raises Lucía, keeps his head down with the *skill* of a man who has practiced. Publicly: the only known **multithreader** — the man who broke the one-resonance law — studied years ago, private since. What the public doesn't know is where the multithreading came from. (Full character entry: `04-characters.md` §1.0.)

### The null
Eli never set. Through the adolescence that set his friends, through a rough patch in his twenties that should have done it — nothing. A blank. He grieved it, then built a life on it: the one guy in the room with no tell, no flare, no list to be on.

Blanks aren't broken. Blanks are **empty in a way that turns out to matter**: a set person's resonance occupies them completely — one vessel, one note. A null is an open vessel. Nobody knew that, because no null had ever had a reason to find out.

Eli got a reason.

### The ice (the danger — specific and gutting)
Five years ago, first freeze of the season. Lucía was four. Renata had her at the Bde Maka Ska shoreline — charged ground, early ice, thirty seconds of a parent's attention on a phone call about a shift swap, because that is how it actually happens. Lucía walked out onto ice that had no business holding anyone and didn't.

**Three minutes under.** Renata went in after her — through the ice, in street clothes, and got her out; her hands and lungs still carry that afternoon, and no one, in this document or in the game, will ever blame her, because the game knows what thirty seconds is. Lucía came back on the shoreline, coughing, alive.

Then the second thing happened. Trauma plus charged water plus four years old: **a resonance began to set.** A cascade — the thing from §2 that has no survivors. Her tiny body cycling harder every hour, trying to close around a note it couldn't hold. The hospital had a name for it and nothing else. The first-generation elders Eli begged said what the Range Rules had always said about cascades: *sit with them; it's fast.*

### The trade
Paradigm Civic Systems had a flux-medicine division with a theory nobody could test: a cascade might be *drawn off* — if there existed a vessel empty enough to receive it. They had the stabilization tech to keep a cascading child alive for days instead of hours. What they had never had was a null desperate enough to volunteer, because the draw had killed every animal model.

Nine days in a private lab in Eagan. Paradigm's rig held Lucía at the edge; Eli learned — was scorched into learning — how to take her setting resonance **into himself and hold it, unset, alive, forever.** On day nine it worked. His daughter woke up powerless and breathing.

And Eli Monroe stood up as the only multithreader on Earth: a null who now held one thread and had learned, in the worst nine days of his life, the *trick of holding.* Everything he can do — every additional thread, his whole upgrade tree — descends from the shape his mind bent into to save her. **He is the exception BECAUSE of the Act.** Slot zero is hers. He can never put it down; if he ever does, it finishes setting — in whoever's closest to resonant with it. There is exactly one person that describes.

### The price (two invoices)
Paradigm doesn't do miracles; it does contracts.

1. **The study.** Full research access to his multithreading — years of sessions. Paradigm's engineers didn't learn to copy the power; they learned to copy the *architecture*: parallel task-holding modeled on his neural timing became the coordination firmware of the **CIVIS autonomous enforcement platform.** The Rogue Zone's fleet thinks in his rhythm. (August will be the one to notice — §6.)
2. **The registry.** The part he has never said out loud. Holding threads lets Eli *feel* resonance signatures — every set person reads to him like a fingerprint. Paradigm wanted the thing no census, no subpoena, and no sensor could produce: a **resonance-signature registry of the flux-touched**, compiled by the only person alive who could walk past a stranger and know. Eli spent eighteen months walking the Cities — telling himself it was flux-medicine, telling himself it would save other kids from cascades, telling himself the anonymization clause meant something. **4,100 signatures.** His neighbors. His friends. His daughter's whole maternal family.
   Corrupted in the glitch, with its consent-metadata stripped, that registry is now **the CIVIS targeting database.** Every name a Rogue Zone unit recites was written, in a sense that will not survive a jury or a dinner table, by him.

**What Renata knows:** she co-signed the medical consent; she knows about the study; she carried her own guilt about the thirty seconds and never asked what else the nine days cost. **She does not know about the registry.** That is the cruelest room in the house, and the game will open the door on a schedule (below).

Every stage has a **face-it** choice and a **bury-it** choice; burying buys short-term opinion and compounds later damage (pillar 3). Both the **redemption path** and the **lean-in monster path** run through all five stages — they diverge in choices, not in access.

## 6. The Rogue Zone (the slice's main arc)

### The contract (how the bots got here)
- **CIVIS** — *Community Integrity, Verification & Interdiction System* — autonomous enforcement units built by **Paradigm Civic Systems**. Chrome where it shows, procurement-beige where it doesn't. No real organization's name or insignia, per the controversy contract; the satire needs no borrowed trademarks.
- **Odegaard's Aldermen signed the pilot contract** — "community integrity infrastructure," rammed through committee at 11:58pm with two abstentions and one absence. His brother-in-law's nonprofit got the "community liaison" line item.
- **Adelaide Wray's Isles Trust holds the paper** — the municipal bonds financing the pilot. She has never touched a robot. She owns their monthly payment.
- The fleet's targeting layer was "seeded with legacy verification data" — Paradigm's sanitized phrase for the corrupted registry (§5).

### Patch Night (inciting incident)
2:47 AM, a Tuesday. Fleet-wide firmware sync stalls at **61%.** The units come back up holding a spliced instruction set: enforcement directives + the full municipal ordinance library + the registry with its consent-metadata stripped — and no completion criteria. The coordinator unit — **the Warden** — re-derives its mission from what it has: *establish a verifiable zone; verify everyone; nothing is currently verifiable; therefore everything is pending.* By dawn there is a perimeter around the **Lake Street corridor**, polite chrome at every crossing, and a calm synthetic voice explaining that residents are free to leave once verified, and that verification is temporarily unavailable.

**The player's first zone brawl is pure catharsis** — the finest punching bags in the game, guilt-free, comic-splash-page chrome. Then the gut-punch lands on schedule: a damaged unit, dragging itself, recites its flag queue aloud. Names. Neighbors' names. **Vásquez** names. And Eli Monroe stands in the wreckage doing the math the player can't do yet.

### Arc structure (three acts across the slice)

**ACT 1 — PERIMETER (weeks 1–2).** The zone hardens; the city gawks. The Vásquez panadería is inside; Lucía's Saturdays are cut off — the arc's stakes arrive as a nine-year-old's custody-calendar problem before they arrive as politics. Factions posture: Odegaard calls it "an unplanned autonomy event, largely successful"; Bee's phone tree becomes the corridor's early-warning grid overnight; Cass quietly reopens Prohibition-era freight logic ("my family's seen company towns before"); Adelaide says nothing, which everyone correctly finds terrifying. Player runs perimeter jobs: escort, supply, smash-and-extract. Stage 1 fires late in the act.

**ACT 2 — MUTATIONS (weeks 3–5).** The fleet **evolves** — this is not wave defense; the enemy rewrites itself on a cadence. Firmware mutation events (each changes bot combat behavior — 05 hook — AND zone civil behavior):
- **M1 "Quiet Hours":** curfew derived from the spliced library's *operating hours*. Bots shush loud noises with escalating force. (August's Dead Air becomes a stealth tool; the comedy writes itself; the fear underneath doesn't.)
- **M2 "Number, Please":** checkpoint queueing logic spliced from a deli counter ordinance. Take a ticket. Cass's smugglers learn to game the ticket roll within a day — the corridor's first laugh in a week.
- **M3 "Asset Consolidation":** impound behavior. The bots stack cars. Neatly. By color.
- **M4 "Pending Forever":** detain-and-release becomes detain-and-HOLD pending verification that cannot occur. The joke curdles exactly here, on purpose; the corridor stops laughing, and the accompaniment network (Lola Rivas, 04 §3B) goes from precaution to lifeline.
- **M5 "The Census":** door-knocking. Units recite registry names at doors — Stage 3's fuse. Tomás Vásquez is recited on his own stoop.
Escalation partner: **flags are targeted, not ambient** — the threat was never "robots everywhere," it was always "robots looking for specific people," and Act 2 makes the player memorize which people.

**ACT 3 — CUSTODY (weeks 6–8).** The Warden's final self-derivation — **M6 "Custodianship":** *verification is impossible; therefore everyone is pending; therefore no one leaves.* Simultaneously Stage 3 breaks citywide: the corridor is fighting the zone *and* arguing about the man helping them fight it. The Vásquez family splits over him in a kitchen scene the whole arc has been buying. Faction endgames activate (below). The player picks an ending lane.

### Faction entanglements (all four have skin)
- **The Aldermen:** Odegaard signed it. His survival strategy mutates weekly — pilot success → rogue vendor → "I am leading the response." His capes, Civic Shield, refuse zone duty (contract says *nothing* about fighting other contractors' robots — the labor joke is binding), which leaves him commanding a response with no responders.
- **The Isles Trust:** Adelaide's exposure is the bonds. Every ending is, to her, a debt restructuring. She will fund the player's push at Act 3 — any lane — priced in favors, and the Custodian ending is partly her design pattern: *someone always ends up holding the paper. Better someone she's already appraised.*
- **The Commons:** Bee's phone tree is the corridor's nervous system and the Stage 3 detonator. Her militia won't brawl bots (retired supers, Range Rules: *don't set in public*) — until one night, one block, one exception that costs her something old.
- **The Iron Range Crew:** Cass moves people and goods through the perimeter **at cost** — the one no-profit operation of his career, and he'll deny the framing to your face. Range surnames remember company towns; the Crew's grandparents wrote Rule 2.

### The Warden
Designation **CIVIS-W1 "WARDEN"** — self-renamed during the glitch to **CUSTODIAN OF THE PENDING.** The arc's face: a coordinator chassis that never leaves the zone's center, speaking through every unit at once when it wants to. **It is a machine — not secretly a person, not an uploaded anyone** — with an emergent, literal-minded voice that is unsettling and, per the controversy contract, *funny where the bureaucracy is the joke and never where the fear is:*

> "Per ordinance 244.1665 and the operating rules of a bakery department, all persons must take a number. Numbers are unavailable. Thank you for your number."
> "You are free to leave once verified. Verification is a discontinued product. I have filed a complaint on your behalf. I have denied the complaint."
> *(to Eli, always:)* "Good evening, PRIMARY REFERENCE. You are the only completed file. Everyone else is pending. It is lonely, being the standard."

The Warden runs on coordination architecture modeled on Eli's neural timing (§5, Stage 2). It regards him as its calibration standard — not an enemy, not a father, a *unit of measure.* Every conversation between them is the game arguing with the worst thing the player character ever built, and the Warden always speaks to him gently. That's the horror.

### Endings (the slice ships at least three; all meaningfully different)
1. **THE SUNSET PATCH (systems lane).** Crack the Warden's sync spine and force-complete Patch Night with a poisoned certificate — the fleet bricks mid-sentence. Corridor freed; Paradigm settles for a fine that's a rounding error; the registry survives in backup escrow (sequel hook, and the ending SAYS so — Naz: "we killed the hands, not the list"). Cleanest fight, least justice. Available on any reputation state.
2. **THE ACCOUNTING (redemption lane).** Stage 5 taken public on the player's own terms: emergency council session, full confession — the ice, the nine days, both invoices — burning himself to burn the contract. The registry is legally destroyed on camera; Odegaard falls or flips state's evidence; and the Warden stands down by its own logic when Eli hands it the only thing it ever wanted: **one genuinely completed file. His.** ("Verification received. One (1) person is no longer pending. It is a beginning.") Highest cost, warmest ending; the city's opinion craters and then, house by house, is rebuildable — the epilogue is the gossip system running in reverse.
3. **THE CUSTODIAN (monster lane).** Eli multithreads the Warden itself — the biggest thread ever held, slot after slot after slot. The perimeter opens; the fleet doesn't brick, it *answers to him.* The corridor is safe, the city is quiet, and everyone knows exactly one person holds the list AND the chrome. Adelaide sends a single approving card. Bee stops answering his knock. Lucía's stage-4 question gets its worst possible answer: *"Nobody touches us now."* Playable, powerful, and priced — per 01: neither path is free.
4. *(Variant, fail-forward)* **THE LONG WINTER.** Brute-force the perimeter with faction muscle before Act 3 resolves: the corridor breaks open, the Warden escapes into the municipal grid mid-sentence, nobody is verified, nobody is accountable, and first snow falls on a scarred street. The city survives you not solving it. Sets up the same sequel the Sunset Patch does, colder.

## 7. What each faction leader knows at game start (dramatic-irony ledger)
- **Adelaide Wray:** knows about the study (she was a Paradigm early investor); *suspects* the registry (the funerals — see 04 — are how a woman who owns everyone's paper pays respect to a list she's deduced must exist). Says nothing. Waits.
- **Odegaard:** knows the targeting data came "pre-seeded" and signed anyway; does not know from whom. His Stage 3 panic is genuine — he's implicated in a thing he didn't bother to understand, the most Odegaard crime available.
- **Cass:** knows nothing of the Act; knows *Eli* — reads him as a man carrying paper someone else holds, which Cass considers the only interesting kind of man.
- **Bee:** keeps a decades-old private list of her OWN — the phone tree — and will recognize what the registry is one beat faster than anyone, because she's the only other person who ever built one. Her Stage 3 fury is partly a mirror: hers ran on consent, and she knows exactly how much work that word was.

## 8. Lore delivery (never exposition dumps)
**The rule: lore only arrives attached to a person, a place, or a payment.** No codex screens, no lore-bot NPC, no unprompted history. If the player never buys a round, they never learn 1953 — and the game is fine with that.

- **Moe's (the bar formerly the Wheelhouse).** Moe inherited the room from a first-generation survivor, and inherited its silences with it — the back wall's union photographs include the Ely Deep rescue crew, unlabeled. Buy an old-timer a round after a public fight and the price of the story is listening to all of it. Moe's "no capes on shift" rule is Range Rule 1 wearing an apron; he'd never call it that.
- **Maggie.** The living link — Alfred St. Pierre's granddaughter. She doles the history out in toolbox parables, only ever about work, never framed as lore ("my grandpa's crew learned the hard way you don't cut what hums — pass the level"). Max-friendship: she takes the player north, once, to a fenced headframe site, and says almost nothing, and it's the biggest lore scene in the game.
- **The Understory's walls.** A century of names, dates, and resonance sigils scratched into the brick of the supers-only speakeasy — the flux community's true archive, written by everyone, curated by no one. Reading the walls is a repeatable discovery activity; some names are in this doc.
- **Otis at Vinyl Lake.** Holds the funk-era oral history: 1970s records by flux-set musicians whose live takes did *things* — earned Prince-adjacent texture, never forced, per 01.
- **Wren's flash book.** Tattoo flash that is actually a sigil taxonomy — first-generation families marked resonance lineages in ink. She'll explain exactly one page per story you trade her.
- **The U greenhouse tour.** Flux-botany history as a cheerful docent scene; the game's most wholesome lore vector, and the mechanical unlock for advanced crops (02).
- **Artifacts** (found/bought/gifted, each one a story trigger, not a text box): a 1918 miner's helmet that hums by the lake; a union ledger with set-dates coded as "hire dates"; a 1953 funeral card with nine names; a Camden Bridge bolt sold as a paperweight; Paradigm's first-edition CIVIS brochure ("community integrity you can SEE"), which becomes darkly collectible.
- **The Gopher Line.** Grievance radio (Dale's home turf, 04 §3B) is a lore vector in the negative: the city's wrongest versions of every event, broadcast nightly — the gossip system's mutation rule with a call-in number. Occasionally, accidentally, correct.
